Week 14 — From Technician to Instructor

The transition from solving technical problems personally to developing technicians who can solve them independently.

An Unexpected Beginning

Week 14 began with the expectation that I would continue technical work, develop the triage-script concept, and provide some informal mentoring.

Instead, I found myself standing in front of an actual classroom.

What I expected to be technicians shadowing me became formal instruction, whiteboards, classroom discussion, floor exercises, technician assessment, remediation, and eventually lead development.

This was no longer ad-hoc mentoring.

It became a combination of:

  • Instructor work
  • Curriculum development
  • Operational coaching
  • Skills assessment
  • Remediation
  • Lead development
  • Shift continuity

The Central Teaching Principle

“You're not diagnosing a ticket. You're diagnosing a problem.”

The information presented in a ticket is only the starting point.

An automated recommendation may represent a downstream symptom rather than the actual source of the failure.

The technician therefore has two responsibilities:

  • Prove what you believe the problem is.
  • Prove that conclusion against what the original diagnosis said.

    • The diagnosis is also not static.

    As new evidence becomes available, the current diagnosis must change

    with it.

    Observe → Diagnose → Act → Validate → Reassess → Update → Continue

    Teach Tools Through Purpose

    One of the strongest lessons from the week was that technicians should not be taught tools as isolated procedures.

    For every tool, I began teaching five questions:
    • What is it?
    • What does it do?
    • What is its job?
    • How do you know it successfully performed that job?
    • When do you use it?

    Knowing how to operate a tool has limited value if the technician does not understand the condition that should trigger its use.

    The objective was not procedure memorization.

    The objective was diagnostic reasoning.

    Teaching Optics Through Concepts

    Rather than asking technicians to memorize isolated optical procedures, I introduced the underlying concept simply:

    Light carrying data.
    That model made several other concepts easier to reason through:

    • Why light levels matter
    • Why cleaning matters
    • Why loopback testing works
    • Why circuit state changes expected behavior
    • Why one-sided versus two-sided optical problems change the next action

    The intention was to give technicians enough underlying understanding that they could reason through situations they had never seen before.

    Assess Connection, Not Recitation

    The technicians entered training at substantially different technical starting points.

    Some had advanced computer or coding experience. Others had strong general technical knowledge but limited triage experience. Others needed additional development in navigation, workflow, or hands-on troubleshooting.

    That made simple terminology questions an unreliable measure of readiness.

    A better question became:

    Can the technician connect one concept to the next?

    Instead of asking someone only what a tool does, I watched whether they could determine:

  • When should I use it?
  • What evidence tells me to use it?
  • What should happen afterward?
  • What does the result tell me to do next?

    • Classroom Understanding vs. Operational Understanding

    Several technicians understood portions of the classroom discussion but did not assemble the complete picture until we entered the data-center environment.

    Once terminology could be connected to physical equipment such as ports, optics, fiber, racks, devices, LEDs, and cable paths, the process became substantially clearer.

    Some technicians then began explaining the process to one another using the same reasoning structure introduced during training.

    That became an important indication that the concept was transferring rather than simply being memorized.

    Gate Independence by Understanding — Not Time

    Technicians were not released simply because they had spent a certain number of hours in class.

    A simple verbal readiness check became useful:

    “What's your ticket?”
    “What's your first step?”

    Those two questions quickly revealed whether a technician understood the workflow well enough to begin working with greater independence.

    Adapt the Cohort to Different Skill Paths

    The differences in technician experience eventually made a single classroom pace inefficient.

    Technicians who demonstrated readiness were released into peer pairs.

    Technicians who were earlier in their skill-development path received additional time on fundamentals and more direct coaching.

    The principle became:

    Do not force every technician to progress at the same pace.
    Give each technician the instruction needed to reach the same operational standard.
    The objective was not to identify stronger or weaker technicians.

    It was to recognize that each person entered training at a different point and might require a different path to reach independent readiness.

    Remediation

    Two technicians required additional development.

    Both progressed successfully and independently closed tickets the same day.

    Another lesson also emerged:

    Sometimes a technician benefits from completing the final portion of development with a different trainer.

    A different explanation or simply another voice expressing confidence can help the technician transition from:

    “I think I understand this.”

    to:

    “I can do this myself.”

    Day-One Readiness — First Major Validation

    Assess → Normalize → Train → Validate

    Week 14 became the strongest real-world validation yet of the training model I had been developing.

    Assess the technician's starting point.

    Normalize the critical baseline gaps.

    Train the individual where development is actually needed.

    Validate that the technician can independently perform the work.

    The goal is not to make every technician identical. The goal is to bring every technician to a common operational standard as efficiently as possible.
    Ten technicians did not represent ten identical starting points.

    They represented different combinations of:

    • Experience
    • Technical knowledge
    • Computer ability
    • Troubleshooting experience
    • Confidence
    • Learning style
    • Equipment readiness

    Training the Leads

    The scope expanded again when three leads began participating in the training process.

    That introduced another level of responsibility.

    I was no longer simply helping technicians learn how to close tickets.

    I was beginning to transfer the reasoning and training methodology to people who would themselves be responsible for supporting other technicians.

    Training the people who will train and support others.

    Operational Output During a Training Week

    Formal training naturally reduced my personal ticket velocity.

    Even so, I closed three older owned tickets and created no new escalations during the week.

    That reinforced an important distinction in how instructor productivity should be measured.

    The relevant metrics were no longer simply:

    How many tickets did I personally close?

    They also included:

    • Technicians trained
    • Independent ticket closures produced
    • Successful remediation
    • Reduction in floor dependency
    • Lead development
    • Knowledge retained after instruction
    The objective of training is not for the instructor to close more tickets.

    The objective is to create additional technicians who can close tickets correctly without the instructor.

    Week 14 Reflection

    I entered the week expecting technicians to shadow me.

    Instead, I found myself standing in front of a classroom.

    By the end of the week, technicians I had trained were independently closing tickets, technicians requiring additional development had progressed successfully, leads had begun participating in the training process, and the cohort had a repeatable troubleshooting workflow they could continue using without me.

    One of the most important lessons was recognizing that technical teaching is not primarily about transferring procedures.

    It is about transferring reasoning.

    A technician should eventually be able to face a condition I have never shown them before and still ask:

    • What do I know?
    • What evidence supports it?
    • What do I need to prove?
    • What tool answers that question?
    • What did the result change?
    • What do I do next?
    When they can do that without me standing beside them, the training worked.

    That is how Week 14 ended.

    What Happens Next?

    Fourteen weeks document the progression.

    The outcome is still unwritten.
